What it actually pays

Sep 12, 2026 · every return statistic for our certified strategy — and the part where the prop-firm math ruins the story

We've published how we test, what we killed, and why most backtests lie. This post answers the question everyone actually opens with: how much money does the thing make?

Two rules for this page. First, no point estimates — every forward number is a distribution, because a single figure is the oldest trick in strategy marketing. Second, we publish the number that hurts: the prop-firm ladder math at the end is the least flattering thing on this website.

3 · Now the prop-firm math, which is where it gets ugly

Backtest returns are not income. To trade this with someone else's capital you have to pass an evaluation, then survive a funded account's rules long enough to withdraw. We simulated both using the strategy's real daily P&L distribution — not a smooth win-rate model — with the rules of Tradeify's Growth 150K product, including the trailing drawdown that keeps rising underneath you (a detail we got wrong once and corrected).

Roughly one attempt in three succeeds, and each funded account costs about $550 in fees to obtain. Fine. Then the funded account has to actually pay you:

The median funded account produces zero dollars at every size we tested. A minority produce real money — the mean is positive precisely because of them.

Read that chart carefully, because it's the crux. Between a third and a half of funded accounts ever generate one payout. The median account earns nothing: the trailing drawdown ends it before the balance clears the payout buffer. Bigger size passes the eval faster but kills the funded account faster too — median account life falls from 267 days at 2 micros to 48 days at 5.

Our certified strategy — 0% overfitting probability, 92% deflated-Sharpe confidence, seven years of out-of-sample validation — is not strong enough to profitably farm this prop-firm ladder from a $2,000 bankroll. At best (5 micros) the mean outcome is roughly breakeven with a 68% chance of ending down. We are publishing this because it is what the numbers say.

Why? An edge of $12.40 per trade is real but thin, and the ladder taxes it three ways: eval fees on every attempt, a rising trailing drawdown that ends most accounts before payout, and a payout buffer that requires a run of profit the strategy produces only occasionally. The firm's rules aren't unfair — they're simply priced above this particular edge.

5 · What we'd actually tell you

Trading it yourself, one to three micros, in your own account: a median of roughly $2,200–2,900 a year per contract, one year in five red, spending 90% of your life in a drawdown, for about 176 trades. That is a real edge and an honest, small business. It will not change your life and it is not supposed to.

Farming prop evaluations with it: the math doesn't work at $2,000 on the product we modeled. Either the edge needs to be bigger, the sizing smarter, or the product cheaper. A cheaper evaluation with a smaller profit target changes this materially — the fee and the target are the two variables that dominate everything.

And the live caveat, stated plainly: every number here is a backtest. Our own 2026 is negative so far — the widest volatility on record is squeezing a range-based stop — and the published research on strategy decay says newly-deployed edges typically lose a quarter to a half of their backtested Sharpe on contact with the future. Take the medians above, haircut them, and then decide.
