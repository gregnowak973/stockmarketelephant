The resolution

Sep 10, 2026 · how $12 of 1-minute data killed our best backtest ever and acquitted our champion

Everything in this series so far ran on 5-minute bars. Every certification, every graveyard verdict, every chart — 5-minute bars, because that's the data we owned. This final chapter is about the day we bought finer data and re-asked every question we thought was settled. One strategy died. One got a retrial and lost anyway. And the champion came out with a better certificate than it went in with.

1 · The backtest that passed everything

A reader-inspired idea — trade reactions at structural price levels (hourly color-flip levels, the prior day's high and low, the opening print), morning window only, two trades a day, tight 20-point stop, 2R target. 2 · Twelve dollars of truth

The strategy's stop was 20 points and its target 40. An MNQ 5-minute bar routinely spans 20–60 points. Which means the entire trade — entry, stop, and target — frequently lived inside a single bar. And a bar is just four numbers. It does not tell you the order in which price visited them.

Here is a real trade, September 26, 2024, drawn from the actual data:

Left: the 10:50 five-minute bar — its low touches the entry, its high clears the target, so the engine scores a win. Right: the same five minutes in order. The high printed at 10:50. The entry didn't fill until 10:52 — two minutes after the price the engine paid us for was already gone. The trade actually stopped out.

We bought the same two years at 1-minute resolution and re-ran the exact same strategy, same window, same configuration:

Two years, identical strategy, identical days. The only difference is whether the engine can see inside the bar.

The full pre-registered grid at 1-minute: CPCV mean −0.61, overfit probability 73%. Not weakened — gone. And here is the uncomfortable lesson: this was not overfitting, so no overfitting test could catch it. PBO, deflated Sharpe, slippage stress, recency checks — all of them validate the consistency of a measurement. Our measurement was consistently wrong. The only cure for a measurement error is a finer instrument.

A rule now lives in our lab notebook: if a strategy's risk unit is comparable to a single bar's range, its backtest is not evidence until it has been re-run at a resolution where the trade cannot hide inside one bar.

3 · The retrial, and the acquittal

New instrument in hand ($9.46 for the full seven years at 1-minute), we re-ran everything — the champion's full 108-configuration certification, the funded-sizing certification, and every graveyard verdict whose shape allowed it. The bias, it turns out, cuts both ways: bar-scale targets get flattered (that's what killed the level strategy), while bar-scale stops get punished. One old verdict — a stop at the opening-range midpoint — flipped from "loses money" to "makes a little," and still lost its case: a third of the champion's risk-adjusted return. Every other verdict held. Not one adoption changed.

And the champion? It went in certified at 85% deflated-Sharpe confidence and came out at 92%:

4 · So what does it actually pay?

The question everyone asks, answered with the 1-minute numbers and no varnish. One micro contract, the champion, seven-plus years:

$16,421 total on one micro, Sharpe 0.68, worst drawdown −$4,567, 1,324 trades at a 51% win rate. The shape is the point: a grind, not a hockey stick.
Year by year. Two flat years, then $3,800–$4,600 in each full recent year — roughly $300–350/month per micro. 2026 is slightly down so far; we publish that too.

Per micro, that's modest by design — this edge is certified, not spectacular. The economics come from three multipliers, each covered earlier in the series:

If those numbers look small next to what strategy vendors advertise, re-read the vendor autopsy. Small, certified, and real compounds. Large, imaginary, and leveraged doesn't.

5 · What the whole series bought

Eight chapters, ~1,900 sessions of real data, three markets, four strategy families, eighteen recorded negatives, two certified positives, two adversarial audits that found real bugs in our own favor, one false champion executed the day before promotion — total data bill for the final chapter's decisive evidence: $12.06.

The graveyard is the product. Every idea in it stays dead, with its evidence attached, so no future version of us — excited, sleep-deprived, newly confident — can quietly resurrect one. What survives a graveyard like that has earned the right to trade.

The champion trades on. The paper campaign is next. And when live results start diverging from these backtests — they will, everyone's do — we'll publish that, too.
