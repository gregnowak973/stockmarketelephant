How the pros actually do it

Sep 11, 2026 · Two Sigma, Renaissance, AQR, Jane Street, Man AHL, Millennium — sourced, tagged, and compared against our own process

We spent this series building a strategy factory in public. A fair question: is our process anything like what a professional quant desk does — or a hobbyist cosplay of one? So we did the research properly: primary sources where they exist (firms' own publications, researcher podcasts, published papers), books where they don't, and honest tags where the truth is folklore. Every claim below carries its confidence level, because a page about research discipline should practice some.

1 · The assembly line every serious firm runs

The clearest published blueprint is López de Prado's "meta-strategy paradigm": a quant firm's real product is not any strategy — it's the process that manufactures strategies, staffed as specialized stations. His claim: "every successful quantitative firm I am aware of applies the meta-strategy paradigm."

The station that defines professionalism is the red one: an independent backtesting function that knows how many things you tried and won't let you iterate against the test set.

2 · Same factory, very different philosophies

Where firms differ is upstream — where ideas come from — and at the top — how survival is governed:

Renaissance found patterns first and demanded explanations second (unexplained signals traded at capped size). Two Sigma's literal tagline is "it starts with a hypothesis." AQR won't touch a signal without an economic story. Millennium barely inspects the research at all — it governs outcomes.

3 · The statistical gates the field prescribes

The published methodology converges on a short list of hard gates — most of them born from the embarrassing discovery that with enough trials, pure noise produces beautiful backtests (about 14 independent tries manufactures an in-sample Sharpe of 1.0 from nothing):

And on transaction costs, one number worth framing: AQR published the analysis of $1.7 trillion of its own live executions — median cost ~6 bps on US large caps — because they consider the cost model important enough to be research in its own right.

4 · Edges rot — the pros plan for it

Published decay measurements. This is why the factory never stops: a firm needs a pipeline of new edges just to stand still, and pre-set retirement criteria so dead strategies don't quietly bleed.

The verdict: on statistical discipline we run the published institutional playbook almost verbatim — because we took it from the same books their researchers wrote. What we cannot replicate is separation: ten thousand datasets, a trillion dollars of execution data to calibrate costs, and above all, an independent human whose job is to kill our favorite idea. Our substitute is process: frozen scripts, pre-registration, and adversarial review. The finale showed that substitute working — and the graveyard shows it working nineteen times.

6 · What we're stealing next

Three institutional practices this research convinced us to adopt, in order:

1. The cost gate, moved to the front. Carver rejects a strategy whose trading costs exceed a third of its pre-cost Sharpe before looking at any backtest. Our RT Levels v1 — 3,000 trades of costs against a thin edge — would have been flagged at the door.

2. Feature research as the discovery tool. Our order-flow dataset has only ever been tested as a bolt-on filter. The AFML prescription is to ask what the features predict under leakage-proof CV first, and only then build strategies from what survives.

3. Pre-set retirement criteria. Before the champion trades live, we will write down — in advance, like everything else — the live-vs-backtest divergence that forces a size-down and the one that forces retirement. Renaissance let validated models run "without interference"; Millennium fires on a number. Both work because the rule predates the drawdown.
